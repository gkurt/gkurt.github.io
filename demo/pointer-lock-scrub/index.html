Pointer lock scrubbing

Drag any of the three labels sideways to scrub its value. They all read the same pointermove deltas — the only difference iswhen, if ever, they callrequestPointerLock(). The range is 0–10,000 at one unit per pixel — several screen widths of mouse travel — so the first one cannot get anywhere near the top in a single gesture.

A held lock always hides the real cursor — the API gives you no say in that — so while one is held a dot marks the spot it was taken from. Pointer Lock is Chromium/WebKit/Gecko-wide;unadjustedMovement (raw, un-accelerated deltas) is Chromium-only and falls back automatically. Esc always breaks a lock — that's the browser's, not mine. Hold Shift while dragging for a 0.2× fine scrub.

3 · Lock at the edge

Starts unlocked, so short scrubs keep the real cursor. Come within 72 px of the window's edge and the lock engages mid-drag; bring the ghost back 220 px inside the window and it hands the pointer straight back, as many times as you like within one gesture. warp measures what each handover costs.

Things the strip above is showing you

  • The strip is your screen. The paler inset block is this window's viewport, the filled marker is the real OS cursor, and variant 3's two bands are drawn inside the block because that's what they're measured against. Watch variant 1: the marker slides straight out of the viewport block — pointer capture does that — and then jams against the end of the strip, which is where the value dies.
  • While locked, clientX/screenX freeze at the lock position and only movementX keeps ticking. That's why the real marker stops and the hollow ghost keeps going: the ghost is just movementX accumulated by hand.
  • runway is how many more units this gesture can produce before the cursor hits the screen edge. It's finite for variant 1, ∞ whenever a lock is held.
  • You cannot keep the real cursor. A held lock hides it, full stop — there is no flag for it and cursor has no say. You could draw a replacement and walk it along withmovementX, but it would be fiction: it would drift from the cursor's actual whereabouts with every pixel, and on release the real one appears somewhere else entirely. So the dot stays put on the lock point, which is the one thing about the pointer that is still true while a lock is held.
  • Releasing mid-gesture costs you a warp. Variant 3 gives the pointer back once the ghost is clear of both edges, but the OS restores the cursor to wherever the lock was acquired — the edge — not to where the ghost had wandered. Thewarp readout is that gap, measured against the first real sample after the release. Nothing can close it: no API moves the OS cursor.
  • Variant 3 arms on the window, not the screen, and the gap between the two is the whole safety margin. Arming at the screen edge would mean asking for the lock at the exact moment there is no room left to fail into — and the requestcan be refused. Triggering at the window edge means the lock is negotiated while the rest of the screen is still runway, and the cursor is never seen wandering outside the page it belongs to.
  • Releasing needs two guards. The 220 px release band is much wider than the 72 px arming band, so scrubbing across the threshold can't chatter; and after a release, re-arming is refused until the restored cursor has left the arming zone under its own steam — otherwise it would land on the edge and re-lock on the very next event. (Chrome's re-lock penalty is real but only applies afterEsc; a programmaticexitPointerLock() is not rate limited.)
  • requestPointerLock() needs a user gesture. Variant 2 asks during pointerdown, which is unambiguous. Variant 3 asks during a pointermove, which relies on the sticky activation from that same drag's pointerdown — Chromium is happy, other engines may reject it, and when they do the rejection shows up in the note under the strip rather than silently doing nothing.
